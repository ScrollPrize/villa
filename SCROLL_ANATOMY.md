# Anatomy of a Scroll

## Scope of this document

This document is not exhaustive. Carbonized scrolls may contain damage or
material arrangements not described here.

The CT appearances described below are possibilities, not diagnostic rules.
A single matching feature does not establish a phenomenon, and the absence of
one described feature does not rule it out.

## Papyrus sheets and their material

This document describes ancient papyrus sheets made from two layers of pith
strips. Within each layer, the strips were laid side by side. The second layer
was arranged roughly perpendicular to the first, and the two layers were
pressed and dried to form a single sheet.

### Sheets and fibers

- SHEET (kollema): A manufactured unit of papyrus formed from two layers of
  pith strips. It is not a winding or the complete roll.

- FIBER: In this document, a fiber is a fibrovascular bundle from a pith strip.
  In an intact strip, the fiber runs along the strip and is embedded in its soft
  ground tissue (parenchyma). Papyrus is not made entirely of fibers. The fiber
  is not the pith strip itself.

### Surfaces and fiber families

- A manufactured papyrus sheet has two outer surfaces: RECTO and VERSO. Each is
  the outer face of one of the sheet's two layers. Each layer has thickness and
  is made of many pith strips. Each strip contains fibers surrounded by
  parenchyma. Neither surface is a single fiber. Where the material remains
  continuous, the surface can be traced by following the material and its
  fibers. Damage may interrupt or separate the material without changing which
  original surface it is associated with.

- In the manufactured sheet, each fiber lies within one of the two layers and
  runs along that layer. It does not pass through the sheet from RECTO to VERSO.

- Damage can separate a fiber from the surrounding parenchyma and from the rest
  of its sheet. The fiber may then move away from the surviving sheet material.
  The detached fiber still belongs to its original sheet and remains associated
  with the same RECTO or VERSO surface. It is not a separate papyrus surface,
  sheet, or winding. If direct continuity has been lost, its relationship to the
  surviving surface must be inferred from the surrounding papyrus geometry.

- The spacing between fibers associated with the same RECTO or VERSO surface
  can vary. Fibers may be widely spaced or grouped closely together, and one
  fiber may split lengthwise into smaller pieces that lie close to one another.
  Two fibers may also lie close together and run in the same direction even
  when they belong to different windings. Distance and direction alone therefore
  do not show which fibers are associated with the same surface. The continuity
  of the surrounding papyrus and the wider surface geometry provide the
  necessary context.

## Roll geometry and windings

### Roll structure

- ROLL: The complete papyrus support made from many sheets (kollemata) joined
  end to end and then rolled up.

- UMBILICUS AXIS: The central longitudinal axis around which the papyrus roll
  was wound.

- WINDING: One turn of the papyrus roll around the umbilicus axis.

- KOLLESIS: The overlapping joint between two consecutive sheets in a papyrus
  roll. It joins the sheets into the continuous roll.

  At a kollesis, two different sheets overlap within the same winding.
  Identifying another sheet there does not by itself identify another winding.

- OPPOSITE SURFACES OF ONE WINDING: The two surfaces that bound the papyrus
  material of a winding at a particular location. Where the geometry permits a
  local measurement, the separation between a sheet’s RECTO and VERSO surfaces
  is measured from RECTO through the sheet to the corresponding VERSO surface,
  perpendicular to RECTO at that location. Before measuring from RECTO, we need
  to identify the VERSO surface of the same sheet; it may not be the first
  papyrus surface we encounter along the direction perpendicular to RECTO.
  The distance includes any internal delamination gap.
  At a kollesis, two sheets overlap within one winding, so these surfaces bound
  the combined overlapping material rather than either sheet alone. The distance
  between these surfaces can change abruptly because of delamination,
  compression, damage, or other deformation. Their distance at one location may
  not match their distance nearby.

- Winding identity comes from the original rolled structure, not from
  present-day material connectivity. Delamination, cracks, and missing material
  can separate parts of one winding, while fusion can connect different
  windings. Connected material can therefore belong to different windings, and
  disconnected material can belong to the same winding. Their original
  relationship must be inferred from the surrounding surface geometry.

- In the original rolled structure, if three consecutive windings occur in the order
  A–B–C, then B lies between A and C. A and B are adjacent, B and C are adjacent,
  and A and C are two winding steps apart. Damage can change their positions,
  so windings that are close together now were not necessarily adjacent in the
  original roll.

- Sheets are the manufactured pieces joined to make the roll; windings are the
  turns formed when the roll is wound. They do not form a containment hierarchy:
  a single sheet can span several windings, and a single winding can contain
  parts of different sheets.

- Each surface of a manufactured sheet is associated with the principal fiber
  family of the layer that forms that surface. Before the sheet was rolled or
  deformed, the RECTO and VERSO fiber families were approximately perpendicular.

- By the convention used here, RECTO fibers run lengthwise in the unrolled roll,
  and VERSO fibers run across its height. In regularly rolled, undeformed
  geometry, RECTO fibers are approximately circumferential around the umbilicus
  axis, while VERSO fibers are approximately axial.

- These directions describe the manufactured sheet and the ideal geometry of a
  regularly rolled, undeformed scroll. The carbonized scroll is not preserved
  in that ideal geometry. Folds, compression, and other deformation can bend or
  rotate a surface locally, sometimes abruptly. After breakage, part of the
  papyrus may move from its original position. In either case, the surface and
  its associated fibers can have directions very different from the ideal axial
  or circumferential direction. In a particular CT view, the fibers may appear
  diagonal or extend out of the viewing plane.

- A fiber family remains associated with its RECTO or VERSO surface even when
  the material is bent, rotated, or displaced. Fiber direction can help
  distinguish RECTO from VERSO, but the axial-versus-circumferential relationship
  is a reference for the broader roll geometry, not a fixed rule for assigning
  RECTO and VERSO at a particular location.

## Following surfaces through the volume

- A fiber traced continuously from one location to another provides evidence of
  local surface continuity. A detached fiber may remain unbroken even where the
  surrounding papyrus surface is interrupted. Fibers running in similar
  directions at two locations do not by themselves show that an unbroken
  papyrus surface connects those locations. The fibers may belong to different
  windings.

- After tracing the RECTO and VERSO surfaces of a sheet in one region, we may
  find more papyrus beyond them in a direction perpendicular to the sheet. This
  does not necessarily mean we have reached a different sheet. The same sheet
  can continue into another winding. It can also fold back on itself, so we
  encounter it again in the same winding.

  Finding another pair of surfaces, with RECTO-associated fibers on one and
  VERSO-associated fibers on the other, does not by itself distinguish these
  possibilities. At a kollesis, an additional pair may belong to the overlapping
  sheet within the same winding. We need the surrounding papyrus geometry to
  determine which sheet and winding the material belongs to.

- An intact fiber follows a continuous three-dimensional course, although CT
  may not resolve all of it. Where visible, a single slice shows only the
  portion or cross-section intersected by that plane; following the fiber
  through a sequence of slices or other coherent planes can reveal more of how
  it runs through space. Where the fiber families are approximately
  perpendicular, a viewing plane perpendicular to the local papyrus surface
  can cut lengthwise through the fibers of one family and crosswise through
  the fibers of the other. One family may then appear as elongated traces and
  the other in cross-section. The plane that gives this view changes
  orientation as the papyrus bends or rotates within the CT volume.

## Delamination, kollesis, folds, damage, and compression

- Delamination can open a gap inside a papyrus sheet. At the gap, the separated
  material still belongs to the same winding; the split does not create another
  winding.

- In delamination, the split lies inside the papyrus sheet, between its RECTO
  and VERSO surfaces. The split can occur between the two layers of pith strips
  or inside one of those layers. RECTO and VERSO still refer to the two outer
  surfaces of the original sheet, not to the sides of the internal gap.

- Only part of a sheet may delaminate. The sheet can remain intact around the
  delaminated region.

- Seeing two parts of the papyrus separate and then come together does not by
  itself show whether they belong to one delaminated sheet or to different
  windings. A delamination can open and close within a sheet, while two
  different windings can touch over a limited region and then separate.

- At a kollesis, two sheets overlap within one winding. The interface between
  the sheets lies inside the overlap. The opposite surfaces of the winding are
  the two outer surfaces of the combined overlapping material.

- The interface between the two sheets in a kollesis can open, making the
  overlapping sheets look locally like surfaces from different windings. They
  still belong to the same winding.

- Fibers can be interrupted by piercings, sharp bends, and fractures that cross
  multiple windings. The end of a traced fiber does not by itself show that the
  papyrus surface ends there.

  When we follow the roll lengthwise across a kollesis, we reach the next sheet
  before the previous sheet ends. Identifying another sheet at the kollesis does
  not by itself identify another winding.

  A tear or kollesis can explain why a traced fiber ends, but only when that
  explanation is supported by the surrounding papyrus geometry.

- A fold bends papyrus back onto itself and can involve one or several windings.
  Folding does not change which winding the material belongs to. The RECTO and
  VERSO surfaces keep their identities through the fold. A surface may face the
  opposite direction after the bend without changing from RECTO to VERSO or
  from VERSO to RECTO.

- A sharp bend is not necessarily a fold. In a fold, the papyrus doubles back
  on itself; compression or another deformation can bend the papyrus sharply
  without making it double back.

- A fold may be confined to part of a winding. Beyond the folded region, the
  papyrus can continue along the winding. Within the folded region, a straight
  line through the papyrus may encounter the same sheet or the same surface
  more than once. Repeated intersections do not by themselves indicate
  different sheets or windings.

- Near the core of a roll, papyrus may fold back on itself one or more times
  rather than following a simple spiral through that region.

- A tear or fracture breaks the papyrus material. The affected surface may end
  at the break or reappear nearby in a displaced position.

- Breakage and movement can disrupt the local arrangement of papyrus surfaces.
  Parts of one or more sheets or windings may shift, separate, fold, or come
  back into contact. Surfaces that are now close together were not necessarily
  adjacent before the damage, and surfaces that originally belonged together
  may now be separated. This movement does not change which sheet or winding
  each surviving part came from. Their original relationships must be inferred
  by tracing the surfaces away from the damaged region and relating them to the
  surrounding sheets and windings. The shape of a break can help, but damage
  can alter it, and unrelated breaks can look alike.

- Missing material is papyrus that has been lost or destroyed at a location. It
  cannot be observed or traced through the missing region. Continuity across
  that region can only be inferred from the surviving surfaces and fibers
  around it and from the wider arrangement of sheets and windings. The evidence
  may not be available immediately around the missing region; following the
  surviving papyrus farther through the volume can help us understand which
  parts were connected.

- Foreign material, such as mineral grains or unrelated debris, may be embedded
  in the papyrus or lodged between windings. It is not itself a papyrus surface,
  sheet, or winding.

- Compression can bring adjacent windings into contact, reduce or eliminate the
  space between them, and make their surfaces appear locally merged in CT. The
  interface may become difficult or impossible to resolve. The loss of a
  separable interface does not by itself show that the surrounding unbroken
  material has changed order.

- When CT shows no visible gap between two papyrus surfaces, it may not reveal
  whether they are separated below the scan resolution, pressed together,
  adhered, or fused. In all these cases, the surfaces remain associated with the
  same original windings. Material from two windings can fuse into one connected
  region, but the windings retain their original identities even when CT cannot
  show where the material from one winding ends and the material from the other
  begins.

- If two papyrus surfaces that are distinct nearby converge into a region where
  CT cannot separate them, this supports the inference that both surfaces are
  still present there.

## CT visibility and contrast

- SCAN ARTIFACT: A feature introduced by CT acquisition or reconstruction
  rather than a physical feature of the papyrus.

- Fibers may remain visible in CT where the surrounding parenchyma has been
  damaged, lost, or become too faint to resolve. Their direction and continuity
  can help trace a papyrus surface. Fiber spacing can vary, so a small
  observation window may contain only one visible fiber. Its relationship to
  the surface must be understood from its continuation and the surrounding
  papyrus geometry.

- CT intensity is a reconstructed image value, not a material label or a direct
  measurement of mass density. It depends on the material, X-ray energy,
  phase effects, and reconstruction. The same CT intensity value need not have
  the same meaning in another scan or processed volume.

- As X-rays pass through material, their waves can shift in phase. Interference
  between these waves can produce bright and dark fringes, or halos, around
  material boundaries in CT. These fringes arise from the imaging process and
  do not represent additional papyrus material.

- Blur can make nearby structures appear merged, so a visible band or profile
  peak need not correspond to one physical structure. Densely packed fibers
  can contribute to this blur: their fine cellular structure may scatter or
  slightly deflect X-rays. The amount of blur depends on the papyrus structure
  and scan conditions.

- A winding is a turn of the papyrus roll, not an intensity band. Neither a
  bright region nor a dark region identifies a fiber, a winding, or empty space
  by itself. A continuous bright or dark region in CT does not by itself show
  that the papyrus is physically connected there, and a gap in that region does
  not by itself show a break in the papyrus. We need the surrounding papyrus
  geometry to interpret the contrast.

- Voxel size is not the same as effective resolution. Whether a structure can
  be distinguished also depends on blur, phase effects, reconstruction,
  contrast, and local preservation.

- At coarser spatial resolution, nearby structures may no longer be resolved
  separately and can appear joined even though they are physically distinct.
  Finer spatial resolution may show that they are separate.

- Fibers are among the most visually distinctive features of the papyrus in CT.
  We can generally follow them through the volume using their shape, course,
  and relationship to the surrounding papyrus. Brightness alone does not
  identify a fiber. Depending on preservation, compression, contrast, and
  spatial resolution, fibers may appear blurred, become difficult to distinguish
  from the surrounding parenchyma and other nearby material, or disappear from
  view locally. Being able to follow a fiber in CT does not mean that its width
  or internal structure can also be resolved.

  Where a fiber is no longer clearly visible, the parts we can still see and
  the surrounding papyrus geometry can help us infer whether it continues and
  what course it follows.

## Ink

- INK: Material applied to a papyrus surface to form writing. It is not one of
  the sheet's two layers of pith strips and does not create another sheet or
  winding. Its presence or absence does not define RECTO or VERSO in this
  document.

- Herculaneum inks are generally carbon-based and may provide little
  absorption contrast against carbonized papyrus. Composition, preservation,
  and scan conditions vary, so ink has no universal CT intensity or appearance.

- Ink may appear as a surface deposit, raised texture, cracked texture
  ("crackle"), or subtler changes in contrast and texture. In some scans it is
  directly visible. None of these appearances identifies ink by itself, and
  their absence does not establish that a surface is uninked.

## Cues that are not diagnostic by themselves

- The distance between two papyrus surfaces tells us only how far apart they are
  at that location. Two nearby papyrus voxels in the CT volume can map to widely
  separated positions in the unwrapped scroll. Even a very small distance does
  not show that they are the opposite surfaces of one winding. How the two
  surfaces continue through the surrounding papyrus provides the context for
  understanding their relationship.

- Similar local orientation does not show that two papyrus surfaces are the
  opposite surfaces of one winding. Surfaces from different windings can also
  be locally parallel.

- Two papyrus surfaces following the same local bend do not necessarily belong
  to the same sheet or winding. Surfaces from different windings can have
  similar local shapes.

- Similar texture or CT intensity does not show that two papyrus surfaces
  belong to the same winding. Surfaces from different windings can look alike.

- Thickness alone is not a reliable topology cue, and a thickness value from
  one region should not be assumed to apply in another.

- The number of peaks in one CT intensity profile does not by itself establish
  the number of material layers or windings.
